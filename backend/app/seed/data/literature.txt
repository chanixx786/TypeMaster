The old lighthouse stood at the edge of the cliff, its paint long since stripped away by decades of salt wind and rain. Fishermen used to say that on the clearest nights, the light still turned, though the keeper had been gone for over forty years. Nobody could explain it, and after a while, nobody tried. It simply became part of the town's rhythm, the way the tide was part of its rhythm, the way the gulls crying at dawn were part of its rhythm.

Elena had grown up hearing these stories from her grandmother, who swore she had seen the light herself as a girl, walking home along the cliff path after a dance that had run too late into the night. She described it not as frightening but as comforting, like a lantern left on by someone who loved you and wanted you to find your way home safely.

Now, as an adult returning to the town for the first time in years, Elena found herself standing at the base of the lighthouse, staring up at its crumbling white walls. The door hung loosely on rusted hinges, and grass had grown thick around the steps leading to the entrance. She hesitated before pushing it open, half expecting the hinges to scream in protest, but they only groaned softly, as if resigned to being disturbed after so long.

Inside, dust hung suspended in the shafts of light pouring through cracked windows. A spiral staircase wound upward into shadow, each step worn smooth by feet that had climbed it long before she was born. She placed her hand on the railing, cool and slightly damp, and began to climb, her footsteps echoing in the empty tower.

At the top, the lamp room was exactly as her grandmother had described it: a great glass lens, cloudy with age, surrounded by a narrow walkway that circled the tower. Through the salt-streaked windows, the sea stretched endlessly toward the horizon, gray and restless under a sky the color of old pewter. Elena stood there for a long moment, feeling the wind press against the glass, and understood for the first time why her grandmother had never been afraid of this place. It was not a monument to loss. It was a promise that someone would always be watching, waiting for the ships to come home.

She reached out and touched the cold glass of the lamp, and for the briefest instant, she could have sworn she felt it hum beneath her fingers, as though something inside were still, quietly, faithfully, alive.
